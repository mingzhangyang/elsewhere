# Scientific conventions and reference checks

Elsewhere is an educational sky explorer, not an instrument for navigation, telescope pointing, or eclipse timing. The accepted input range is January 1, 1900 through December 31, 2100 UTC. Local calendar dates at those boundaries can fall in the adjacent year. A supported input range is not a claim of independently established accuracy throughout that range.

## Coordinates and time

The domain stores one UTC instant and a separate IANA time zone. Location changes preserve the instant. Temporal resolves local dates, rejects nonexistent spring-transition times, and exposes both offsets of a repeated hour. The timeline follows actual local midnight boundaries, including 23-, 25-, and fractional-hour days.

Observer latitude and longitude are WGS84 degrees north/east. Elevation is meters above sea level. Presets/search supply elevation where available. Device geolocation deliberately assumes sea level: browser altitude is ellipsoidal and cannot be passed as mean-sea-level height without a geoid conversion. The location label discloses this assumption. Time zones come from the coordinates, independently of weather availability.

Azimuth is clockwise from north. Displayed Sun, Moon, planet, and star altitudes are apparent: they include Astronomy Engine's standard refraction (about 0.5° at the horizon, about 1′ at 45°). The Sun, Moon, and planets also carry `geometricAltitude`, which excludes refraction, for reference comparisons. Rise/set uses its limb and atmospheric conventions. Daily events are filtered to the selected civil day. Polar day/night and days missing one event are distinct states. The unobstructed horizon is mathematical; the optional ridge is illustrative.

## Circular sky-chart projection (SM2-A)

`src/domain/skyChartProjection.ts` defines a pure **azimuthal equidistant** projection of the hemisphere above the mathematical horizon. It consumes the standard-refracted **apparent altitude of an object's center** and its azimuth, both in degrees. Azimuth increases clockwise from north. On a unit-radius disc with zenith at `(0, 0)`, `r = (90 - altitude) / 90`, `x = -r * sin(azimuth)`, and `y = -r * cos(azimuth)` (trigonometric inputs in radians). Screen `x` is positive right, screen `y` positive down: **north is up, east left, south down, west right**. This differs from the unchanged perspective-camera projection in `src/moment/projection.ts`.

The center at altitude `0` degrees is on the included rim; a center below it returns `null`, regardless of Sun/Moon `visible` flags. Finite azimuths wrap modulo 360 degrees. Non-finite numbers and altitudes outside the physical `[-90, 90]` degree interval throw `RangeError`, never silently clamp; zenith is the exact origin despite its undefined physical azimuth. Floating-point checks use approximately `1e-10` absolute coordinate tolerance (and `1e-12` radial tolerance), not strict exact equality for trigonometric cardinal values.

`tests/skyChartProjection.test.ts` verifies S01/S02 using independently specified synthetic zenith and cardinal coordinates, plus diagonal quadrants, intermediate radii, hemisphere-neutral axes, below-horizon filtering, angle periodicity and invalid inputs. Those geometric tests **do not** establish independent historical astronomical accuracy. SM2-B implements only independent object selection; sky snapshots and catalog readiness orchestration (SM2-C), crescent orientation and rendering remain unimplemented.

## Memorial sky-chart selection policy (SM2-B)

`src/domain/skyChartSelection.ts` exposes the pure `selectSkyChartObjects(candidates, options?)` function and typed `SkyChartCandidates`, `SkyChartSelectionOptions`, `SkyChartSelection`, and discriminated `SkyChartSelectedObject` result. It consumes **already-calculated** apparent (standard-refracted) **center** altitude and azimuth in degrees from the existing `SkyState`/star positions; it does not compute astronomy, query weather, load a catalog, project points, or consult the browser. The caller must explicitly supply the catalog readiness flag. Selection output contains stable IDs, object kinds and unaltered altitude/azimuth, plus star name, magnitude and color index; its order is Sun, Moon, Mercury/Venus/Mars/Jupiter/Saturn, then stars sorted by exact ID. There is no source-object mutation or retained input reference.

Every body is selected if and only if its **center apparent altitude is >= 0 degrees**. Altitude exactly 0 is included; any negative altitude is excluded. This is the same physical boundary and coordinate convention as the unchanged SM2-A `projectSkyChart` function. Geometric/airless altitude, limb-based Sun/Moon `visible`, planetary `visible`, terrain, artistic ground and magnitude do **not** replace this test for Sun, Moon or the five planets. Stellar selection additionally requires `magnitude <= limitingMagnitude`, inclusively. The **default limit is 6.0** for this diagram only; custom limits must be finite in **[-5, 20]** (inclusive), or a `RangeError` is thrown. This threshold never changes positions, magnitudes, point sizes, illumination or the explorer's preferences.

The memorial diagram describes calculated positions, **not unaided-eye observability**. Daylight and solar altitude, full Moon/moonlight, city light pollution, clouds, precipitation, haze and other weather have **no input to the selector**. In particular, it does not import or modify `starVisibility.ts`: the existing explorer independently applies twilight extinction, environment-dependent limiting magnitude, moonlight and weather, and retains its eye-safety messaging. Later artwork rendering must explicitly label daytime diagrams as positional illustrations and retain solar observation safety guidance; selecting a Sun-adjacent body is **not** advice to look at it.

Malformed or incomplete inputs throw, rather than yielding an apparently valid empty chart: both Sun and Moon positions, both planet/star arrays and a boolean `starCatalogReady` are required. Positions must have finite apparent altitude in [-90, 90] and finite azimuth (finite angles beyond 360 remain legal, as in SM2-A). Non-finite/non-numeric stellar magnitudes, invalid optional color indices, empty/duplicate IDs (including objects excluded by magnitude or horizon), missing object fields and unknown planet IDs are rejected. Planet arrays may intentionally be partial and star arrays may be empty **only when explicitly ready**; the caller is responsible for providing its intended complete calculated body set. `starCatalogReady: false` raises `SkyChartCatalogNotReadyError`, **not** an empty selection. This flag is an explicit caller assertion, not proof that a complete catalog was loaded. SM2-C must use `starCatalogLoaded()` and coordinate async loading, calculate the complete `SkyState`, and prevent false success from missing resources. This module does not attempt that lifecycle.

`tests/skyChartSelection.test.ts` contains synthetic exact-boundary, custom-threshold, deterministic order, invalid-input, readiness, hemisphere-neutral and SM2-A unit-disc integration tests. It also includes **counterexamples** where a daytime or urban/full-Moon star is selected for the diagram but has zero explorer visibility by the _existing_ `starVisibilityFactors` model. These are synthetic policy checks, **not** independent astronomical positions or research-grade historical ephemeris validation. The fixture register marks S03's horizon-selection rule and S04's daytime policy separately from their pending real-scene/independent-reference checks. S01/S02 remain SM2-A geometric evidence; S05-S07 remain open.

## Moon and stars

Sun and Moon angular sizes use observer distance. When **Enlarge Moon in wide views** is on (the default, switchable in Layers), the rendered Moon disc has an approximately 8 CSS-pixel minimum (capped at 5× physical screen size on very short views); it returns to the unmodified physical projection as soon as that reaches 8px. Switching the aid off renders the physical disc at every zoom. This is a display aid, not a change to the reported angular diameter, position, illumination, or phase. The Moon's map detail still follows its physical projection. Moon illumination is computed at the observer using Moon-to-Sun and Moon-to-observer vectors. The icon uses the apparent bright-limb angle; the scene uses that geometry plus the IAU lunar pole and prime meridian to orient the surface map. Lunar phase names and waxing/waning use geocentric elongation. Lunar distance is geometric and topocentric, displayed approximately to 100 km. Lunar eclipses and atmospheric differential refraction across the disc are not modeled.

The 5,070-star HYG derivative applies J2000 proper motion and precession, evaluated once per UTC day (they move a star by well under 1″ in a day). Each instant then turns the whole catalog with one equatorial-to-horizontal rotation; the renderer applies that rotation, refraction, and the visibility model per star on the GPU, generated from the same constants as the CPU reference. Star altitude applies the same standard refraction as the Sun and Moon, so low stars keep their places beside them; the airless value is available from `horizontalFromEquatorial`. Annual parallax, nutation, radial motion, and variable brightness are omitted. Magnitude, color, atmospheric extinction, moonlight, and light pollution affect approximate display brightness. These are not calibrated radiometric quantities. Light pollution and moonlight set the limiting magnitude, which decides which stars are drawn; the display scale then keeps every drawn star, down to sixth magnitude in a dark sky, bright enough to see on a screen, and gives stars and planets brighter than magnitude +2 a soft halo that grows with brightness. The scale and the halo are illustrative and do not change which objects are visible. The Milky Way is a procedural glow, not an image or a survey map: the J2000 equatorial-to-galactic rotation (Hipparcos) acts on J2000 directions, while the sphere is precessed to the same UTC-day equator-of-date frame as the stars before being placed in the local sky, and an illustrative model paints the band from those coordinates, brightest and widest toward the center in Sagittarius, with Cygnus and Carina as secondary peaks, the bulge, noise for star clouds, and the dark Great Rift from Cygnus to Ophiuchus. It turns with the stars and fades toward the horizon. Its brightness follows the stars' limiting magnitude, fading out between magnitude 6 and 4, so it is full in a dark moonless sky, faint in a suburban one, gone from a city sky and washed out by a high bright Moon; it needs a darker sky than the stars during twilight, and haze dims it like the stars. Four original simplified line patterns are educational overlays, not a complete constellation atlas.

## Planets

Mercury, Venus, Mars, Jupiter, and Saturn come from Astronomy Engine. Positions are topocentric and apparent (light-time and aberration, equator of date), turned into altitude and azimuth with the same standard refraction as everything else. Range is the light-time-corrected distance, as Horizons reports it; Astronomy Engine applies aberration as a shift of the position vector, which would otherwise change the length by up to about 1 part in 10,000 (some 140,000 km for Saturn). Apparent size uses each planet's equatorial radius (IAU 2015, as Horizons uses). Magnitude, illuminated fraction, phase angle, and elongation are geocentric; the observer's offset changes them by far less than they are displayed. Magnitude follows Astronomy Engine's photometric models, including Saturn's rings, rather than the Mallama and Hilton (2018) models Horizons uses. Rise and set refer to the planet's center on the refracted horizon, filtered to the selected civil day like the Sun and Moon.

Planets are drawn as points through the star shader, so refraction, extinction, the limiting magnitude, light pollution, moonlight, and haze treat them exactly like stars of the same magnitude; point size and brightness use the star scale. Twilight is the one difference. Stars share one fade between Sun altitudes of −18° and −3°. A planet's fade moves toward brighter skies by 1.5° of Sun altitude for each magnitude it is brighter than +1, so Venus at −4.5 is fully faded in at about −10° and still faintly drawn at about +5°, and Jupiter at −2.7 at about −12° and +2.5°. This is an illustrative display model, not a calibrated contrast threshold. The Objects panel text uses the same model as the picture. While the Sun is above the horizon, a planet within 15° of it carries an eye-safety warning in place of any other explanation, however faintly it is drawn; once the Sun has set, a planet within 15° that daylight still hides is described as lost in its glare. Planet tints are illustrative. Discs, phases on screen, Saturn's rings, Jupiter's moons, and conjunction or occultation timing are not drawn; Mercury's and Venus's phase appears in the object details.

## Atmospheric presentation

The Moon's shader adds the same foreground atmospheric color as the surrounding sky to its attenuated, sunlit surface. This prevents the unlit hemisphere from becoming a black silhouette in daylight. Surface lighting, earthshine, haze, cloud coverage, and ground colors are illustrative rather than calibrated radiometry. The curved procedural cloud deck attenuates celestial objects through one shared foreground pass; it is not a measured cloud map. The position marker remains visible through clouds and is explicitly labeled as a calculated position. Visibility explanations use broad conditions, not a claim that the selected sightline is cloud-free. Clear-sky preview changes only the rendered atmosphere and keeps original weather values available.

## Independent regression fixtures

Checked on November 13, 2026 against cached responses from [JPL Horizons](https://ssd-api.jpl.nasa.gov/doc/horizons.html) and the [US Naval Observatory](https://aa.usno.navy.mil/data/api.html). The [Horizons manual](https://ssd.jpl.nasa.gov/horizons/manual.html) defines the reference quantities. Raw responses and query URLs are retained in `tests/fixtures`; tests do not access the network.

JPL DE441 samples cover New York, Sydney, and Tromsø on March 20, June 21, November 24, and December 21, 2026. The comparison is topocentric and airless. Lunar range uses separate simultaneous geometric vectors: Horizons observer-table range includes light-time, while Astronomy Engine's lunar vector is geometric. Comparing those directly would manufacture a range error. The remaining range difference reflects the compact lunar model and is accommodated by explicit display precision and a regression budget.

USNO event samples cover New York and Sydney on January 15, 2026, at sea level with matching local UTC offsets. They include sunrise, sunset, solar transit, civil dawn/dusk, and moonrise/moonset. The reference is rounded to minutes.

Domain tests cover polar and DST semantics, but broader independent review remains a release qualification item.
